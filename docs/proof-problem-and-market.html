AI research needs evidence you can check.

A proposed focus for Proof: help students and professionals verify the academic sources and claims in AI-assisted research.

Proof helps students and professionals check AI-assisted research against academic papers, with source passages they can inspect and clear labels when evidence is missing.

Proposed positioning. Public, redacted version. Private classroom details and operational information omitted.

The problem

Students and professionals use AI to find research, explain papers, and draft evidence-based work. But a convincing answer can contain invented references, inaccurate citation details, or claims that the cited paper does not support. Finding a paper also does not establish that the tool accessed its full text or interpreted its findings correctly. Users still need to check the original evidence before relying on the output.

Published experiments have documented fabricated references and bibliographic errors in AI-generated literature reviews. A 2025 study tested GPT-4o with straightforward prompts on six mental-health topics. Its findings establish a real failure mode, not an error rate for every current AI tool or workflow. Study

Finding and accessing papers

An answer generated without retrieval may name a plausible paper that does not exist. Search-enabled tools can find academic work, but source coverage, paywalls, and incomplete retrieval can limit the evidence available for a check.

Hallucinations and unsupported claims

A reference can be real while the claim attached to it is wrong. AI may invent a result, overstate an effect, or omit a qualification. A link or a correctly formatted citation cannot resolve that by itself.

An AI-assisted report says "The intervention doubled productivity."

The paper reports an 18% improvement on one measured task.

The source exists, but it does not support that sentence. Illustrative example, not a real study.

The job is specific: help me check whether the research is real, whether the relevant evidence is accessible, and whether it supports the claim I plan to use. An unresolved reference should remain unresolved until checked. Proof should never infer fabrication solely because a lookup failed.

Use the precise claim. AI can search academic papers. Elicit and Scite already offer scholarly research tools. The problem is unreliable discovery, incomplete access, and unsupported interpretation. OpenAI also acknowledges hallucinations and incorrect inferences in its deep-research launch documentation. Elicit, Scite, OpenAI documentation

The market

The target market is students and professionals who use AI for work that depends on academic evidence. Their shared need is to verify research before it enters an assignment, publication, client report, or recommendation.

Professional starting point

Test small consulting and research teams that repeatedly deliver reports citing scholarly papers. The proposed value is less time checking references and catching unsupported claims before a report reaches a client or colleague.

Student starting point

Test students completing research-intensive assignments where this assistance is permitted. The proposed value is identifying questionable sources and evidence gaps while preserving the student's own analysis and writing.

AP Seminar can remain an early learning setting. It does not define the market. The stronger shared target is people who use AI for research and are accountable for the evidence in the result.

Proof's proposed role

Begin with the user's existing research or draft. Resolve identifiable references, retrieve available academic evidence, and compare claims with the source text. Show the passages behind each finding and distinguish accessible evidence from missing or incomplete evidence.

The commercial promise to test is a useful evidence check that fits between AI-assisted drafting and human review. It must earn trust through measured performance. Proof also uses AI, so another model's agreement cannot establish that a claim is correct.

This is a proposed product focus, not a claim of exhaustive coverage or professional readiness. Current source-access and extraction limits still apply. Results cannot certify truth, research quality, or the absence of hallucinations.

The competitive position

Elicit offers research discovery and evidence-extraction workflows. Scite offers research answers, citation context, and reference checks. Recite checks citation consistency and style. Proof's proposed distinction is reviewing the claims in an existing piece of AI-assisted work against inspectable academic evidence. This overlaps with existing tools and needs direct comparison in user testing. Elicit, Scite, Recite

Measure whether Proof catches useful errors and reduces review effort beyond the tools users already have. That is a stronger basis for adoption than a general promise to eliminate hallucinations.

What still needs validation

  1. Observe both students and professionals checking real, consented AI-assisted work. Separate fabricated references, incorrect metadata, inaccessible sources, and unsupported claims.
  2. Have qualified readers assess findings against the original papers. Measure false reassurance, useful flags, unresolved checks, and time saved against manual review and existing tools.
  3. Test a concrete paid offer with individual users and professional team buyers. Track repeat use across assignments or reports before choosing a commercial model.

Size the reachable market from identified research workflows, reachable users or teams, tested adoption, and tested prices. The whole student population, all professionals, and the global AI market would overstate the opportunity.
